Rack Master Studio

A client self-service pallet-rack configuration and quote-intake application. Clients do the early configuration work themselves inside controlled guardrails; McMurray Stern receives a structured, revision-pinned submission and keeps its catalog, BOM, pricing, estimating and engineering workflow behind a hard boundary.

Scope fence — read first

This blueprint describes a preliminary planning and quote-request tool. Rack Master Studio is not an engineering-certification or code-compliance product. Nothing it produces asserts structural approval, manufacturer approval, PE approval, fire-protection approval, code compliance, or AHJ approval. Where a question requires an authority the product does not hold, the product's job is to name the gap and stop, not to answer.

01Executive overview and the business problem

What is happening today

A client asks for preliminary rack-layout options. A building with three units and two schemes per unit is six layouts. Each one is drawn by hand by an internal designer: bay sizing, beam elevations, aisle widths, flue and column resolution, a plan, an elevation, a pallet-position count. That is days of skilled drafting work, performed before anyone has committed to buying anything.

Two things then go wrong, and both cost real money:

  • The work is unpriced. Six preliminary options is a design engagement billed at zero. It is absorbed as a cost of quoting.
  • The work is portable. A drawing produced to win the job travels. A client can and does take our plan, our bay sizing and our pallet count to another vendor and ask them to price it. We paid to design the job and someone else quotes it.

The shift

Rack Master Studio moves the early configuration work to the client, and keeps the valuable work inside. The client explores options in a tool that will not let them produce nonsense; we receive a structured package that is far more useful to estimating than an email; and the thing the client can walk away with is deliberately less transferable than a finished drawing.

Client gets

Fast, self-service option exploration; a preliminary plan and elevation; a pallet-position count; a clear list of what is still unknown; a real submission channel.

We stop giving away

Manufacturer part numbers, catalog source detail, capacity tables, BOM quantities, procurement-ready exports, pricing, margin, internal notes.

We gain

Structured intake instead of a phone call; an internal BOM/takeoff derived on arrival; assumptions and review flags written down; a revision history that survives the argument six months later.

We keep

The design authority. Every client-facing output is stamped preliminary and is explicitly not an installation drawing, an engineered design, or an approval.

The value-protection argument, stated plainly

The protection is not achieved by watermarking a drawing. It is achieved by what the client is never given, and that is an architecture decision, not a UI decision:

  • The client sees geometry and counts. They do not see the parts that realise that geometry.
  • A preliminary plan without a bill of materials, without part numbers, and without capacities is meaningfully harder to hand to a competitor as a quote package than a drawing with a schedule on it.
  • Client-visible output is generated from a different projection of the same revision than the internal package — see §9 and Figure 6. Internal fields are not filtered out late; they live in tables a client-facing query cannot reach.
Honest framing

This raises the cost of shopping our work; it does not make it impossible. A determined client can retype dimensions into another vendor's form. What the product buys is that they must re-do the configuration work themselves, rather than forwarding a PDF we paid to produce. That is the realistic goal, and the roadmap should not promise more.

Why a model-first product, and not a drawing tool

The four prior projects (§17) collectively demonstrate one thing: the moment the screen and the calculation disagree, everything downstream is untrustworthy. rack-takeoff keeps its state in DOM input values and triplicates its math across three HTML files. rack-studio has a typed package tree and an independent single-file prototype that implements the same domain differently. In both cases a change in one place silently fails to reach the other.

Rack Master Studio therefore has one canonical, revision-pinned project model. The plan preview, the elevation, the validation results, the assumptions, the BOM/takeoff, the client package, the internal package, the documents and the audit history are all derived from the same revision. There is no screen-only state. A configuration change updates every derived output or it updates none of them.

The governing principle, carried forward

From rack-engine/CLAUDE.md, worth keeping verbatim as product policy:

Product constitution

Never guess. Preserve provenance. Surface uncertainty. For an engineering system an explicit UNKNOWN, OFF_GRID, MISSING INPUT or BLOCKED is substantially better than a plausible but unsupported number.

In a client-facing product this principle gets harder, not easier. A blank in an internal tool is an estimator's cue to go find the number. A blank in a client-facing tool is a support ticket. The design answer is not to fill the blank — it is to name what is missing, say who can supply it, and let the client submit anyway with the gap recorded. See §11.

What success looks like at the end of MVP-1

  1. An internal user invites a named client contact at a named organization. No public signup exists.
  2. That client enters a building, one or more units, and their operational requirements.
  3. They build two controlled selective-pallet-rack options, see a preliminary plan and elevation, and see exactly what is missing or assumed.
  4. They select one and submit it for quote.
  5. The submitted revision is frozen, hashed, and cannot be edited by anyone — client or internal.
  6. An internal user opens it, sees an internal-only BOM/takeoff with every quantity traced back to a rule and a catalog release, and creates a derived working revision without touching the client's record.

Everything else — automated pricing, DWG/DXF export to the client, engineering calculations, multi-vendor catalogs, CAD editing, public self-signup — is explicitly out of MVP-1. See §15 and §19.

Why the actor type is a separate axis from the role

Organization isolation stops Client A seeing Client B. It does nothing to stop Client A seeing our margin on Client A's own project — those rows legitimately belong to Client A's organization. Two orthogonal dimensions are therefore required: organization_id (which rows) and actor_type ∈ {client, staff, service} (which tables and which fields). Conflating them is the single most common way a product like this leaks. See §14.

03Client workflow, internal workflow, and lifecycle

3.1 The client workflow

  1. Invitation. An internal user creates the client organization, creates the project, and invites a named contact by email. The invitation carries the organization, the role and the project assignment on the server side. There is no signup form to find.
  2. Facility and units. The client describes the building: address, clear height, column grid if known, dock locations if known, and then one or more units (a tenancy, a room, a zone). Every field they cannot answer is recorded as Missing input rather than defaulted.
  3. Operational requirements. Pallet dimensions and weight, storage type, handling equipment and its aisle requirement, target pallet positions, sprinkler condition, commodity class if known.
  4. Options. Within each unit the client builds one or more configuration options — in MVP-1, selective pallet rack only, with the option template constrained to a small set of controlled parameters. The tool refuses geometry it cannot support rather than silently approximating it.
  5. Live preview. Every change re-derives the plan, the elevation, the pallet-position count, the assumptions list and the findings list. Nothing is cached that the model has not produced.
  6. Compare. Options are compared side by side on positions, footprint used, aisle width, levels, and the count of open findings. Never on cost.
  7. Submit. The client selects one or more options and submits a request for quote. The submitted revision is frozen at that instant.
  8. Track. The client sees status, any request for information, and the eventual disposition. They can clone their submission to a new draft at any time; the frozen record never changes.

3.2 The internal workflow

  1. Intake. The submission arrives as a structured package: facility and unit inputs, the selected option(s), the derived plan and elevation, the full assumption register, the findings register, the pinned catalog and rule releases, and an internal BOM/takeoff derived from that exact revision.
  2. Triage. An internal user reviews the findings first — blockers, missing inputs, assumptions the client accepted, and anything flagged as requiring external review.
  3. Disposition. One of four: Quote · Request information · Refine internally · Decline.
  4. Derived internal revision. Refining means cloning the frozen submission into an internal revision. The internal revision is a different lineage: the client cannot see it exists, and changes there never propagate back to the client's record.
  5. Quote. Out of MVP-1. When built, a released internal revision is the only thing that can produce a contractual document.

3.3 Revision lifecycle and status vocabulary

Two vocabularies are borrowed rather than invented, because both are already understood by the people who will read our output.

  • Revision and iteration from PDM practice (PTC Windchill's definition): a version is REVISION.ITERATION; the iteration increments on every save, the revision is a deliberate identity change. P01.04 is revision P01, fourth save.
  • P / C prefixes from BS EN ISO 19650-2 (UK National Annex): P = Preliminary, non-contractual; C = Contractual. Client-facing revisions are always P. Internal released revisions are C. Two industries — AEC document control and PLM — converged on the same structure, which is a good sign it is the right one.

05Non-functional requirements

5.1 Security

  • NFR-SEC-01 — All traffic TLS 1.3 (1.2 for compatibility); TLS 1.0/1.1 disabled per RFC 8996. HSTS on every response. App-to-database connections use sslmode=verify-full.
  • NFR-SEC-02 — Internal staff authenticate via OIDC SSO with phishing-resistant MFA (passkeys/WebAuthn). No local passwords for anyone who can see internal data.
  • NFR-SEC-03 — Client users: password (NIST SP 800-63B-4 compliant: 15-character minimum for single-factor, 8 with MFA; no composition rules; no forced rotation; full-password breach-list check) plus mandatory TOTP or passkey. Email magic links are not an acceptable standing second factor — NIST's own FAQ states email SHALL NOT be used as an out-of-band authenticator.
  • NFR-SEC-04 — Target AAL2. Internal sessions: 8-hour absolute, 30-minute idle. Client sessions: 24-hour absolute, 2-hour idle. Opaque server-side session tokens in __Host- prefixed, Secure, HttpOnly, SameSite cookies with ≥128 bits of entropy. Never localStorage.
  • NFR-SEC-05 — Session identifier regenerated on authentication and on any privilege change. Server-side revocation is mandatory; disabling an account terminates every session.
  • NFR-SEC-06 — Secrets live in a managed vault, never in source, config files, or environment variables. Secret scanning in CI.
  • NFR-SEC-07 — Deny-by-default routing: a route with no declared authorization policy fails at application boot, not at request time.

5.2 Data isolation

  • NFR-ISO-01 — Shared database, organization_id on every tenant-scoped row, Postgres row-level security on every such table, and the tenant predicate written explicitly in application queries. The two controls fail independently: RLS fails silently (empty results), application predicates fail loudly in tests.
  • NFR-ISO-02 — Every tenant-scoped table has ENABLE plus FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY and at least one policy covering SELECT, INSERT (WITH CHECK), UPDATE and DELETE. Asserted by a CI test that reads pg_class.relrowsecurity and pg_policy for every table in the application schema.
  • NFR-ISO-03 — The application database role owns nothing and has neither SUPERUSER nor BYPASSRLS. Migrations run as a separate owner role.
  • NFR-ISO-04 — Tenant context is set with set_config('app.organization_id', $1, true) inside an explicit transaction, never a session-scoped SET. Under a transaction pooler a session-scoped SET is a cross-tenant leak waiting for a load spike. Enforced by a single withTenant() wrapper; raw connection checkout is banned by lint.
  • NFR-ISO-05 — Every uniqueness constraint on tenant data is composite with organization_id. Foreign-key and unique checks bypass RLS entirely, so a global unique index leaks the existence of another tenant's rows through constraint-violation errors.
  • NFR-ISO-06 — Denials for cross-tenant object access return 404, never 403.

5.3 Auditability

  • NFR-AUD-01 — Audit events are written in the same database transaction as the change they describe. A change without its audit record must be impossible, not merely unlikely.
  • NFR-AUD-02 — The audit table is append-only: UPDATE and DELETE revoked from the application role plus a trigger that raises. Sealed batches ship to object storage with WORM retention.
  • NFR-AUD-03 — Events are hash-chained (hash_n = SHA-256(prev_hash ‖ canonical(event_n))) and the head hash is externally anchored daily.
  • NFR-AUD-04 — Ordering authority is a monotonic sequence, not a timestamp. Both occurred_at and recorded_at are stored; a large gap between them is itself alertable.
  • NFR-AUD-05 — Audit logs and application logs are separate pipelines. Application logs may be sampled and rotated; audit events may not.
  • NFR-AUD-06 — Retention: audit events 7 years in WORM, 13 months hot and queryable; application logs 30–90 days. Written down, and followed.

5.5 Backups and recovery

  • NFR-BK-01 — Point-in-time recovery on the primary database with a documented RPO of 5 minutes and RTO of 4 hours.
  • NFR-BK-02 — Submission manifests and generated documents live in object storage with versioning and Object Lock; they are not recreated from the database on restore, they are the record.
  • NFR-BK-03 — A restore is tested quarterly and the test produces an artifact. An untested backup is not a backup, and it is the first thing an auditor asks for.
  • NFR-BK-04 — Dump and ETL jobs run with SET row_security = off so a silently truncated backup is impossible — that setting errors rather than filtering.

5.6 Observability

  • NFR-OB-01 — Structured logs with a request correlation id propagated through every service call.

06Recommended web architecture and rationale

6.1 The shape

A conventional server-rendered/SPA web application on a managed Postgres, with one unconventional and load-bearing decision: the derivation kernel is a single pure module, compiled once, that runs unchanged in the browser and on the server. 07Canonical data model

7.1 The rule the model exists to enforce

Architecture principle

There is one canonical, revision-pinned project model. The plan and elevation preview, the validation results, the assumptions, the BOM/takeoff, the client package, the internal quote package, the generated documents and the audit history are all derived from the same revision. There are no independent screen-only models. A configuration change either updates every derived output or it updates none of them.

Concretely this means the revision is the only thing that is stored as fact. Everything downstream is a function of it:

derive(revision) → { layout, findings, assumptions, bom, displayList }

Derived rows are persisted (they are expensive and we need them queryable), but they are caches with a provenance key, not sources of truth. Each derived row carries the revision_hash and engine_version it was produced under, and any row whose key does not match the current revision is invalid and is recomputed rather than displayed. This is the mechanism that makes "no screen-only model" enforceable rather than aspirational.

7.3 Value representation

Three conventions, all lifted from the reference projects because they were the hardest-won lessons there:

  1. Fixed-point integers, stored in micrometres. Lengths are integer µm; loads and capacities are integer millipounds. Division that is not exact is refused; quantities that must be split are allocate()d so the remainder goes somewhere explicit rather than into floating-point drift. rack-studio/packages/units is a complete, 100%-branch-covered implementation of the pattern, based on mil — the base changes, the mechanics do not.
  2. Why micrometres and not millimetres. One inch is 25.4 mm exactly, so every published inch value is a whole number of µm (48″ = 1,219,200 µm; the 5.92″ beam face = 150,368 µm) and every whole millimetre is a whole number of µm. Integer millimetres cannot do this: 48″ is 1219.2 mm, and rounding it to 1219 reads back as 47.9921″, which no longer matches the published lookup key. 18 of the 21 published spans miss their own key under integer-mm storage, silently turning every lookup off-grid and destroying the one behaviour the engine is built around (§10.4). Micrometres give a metric base with none of the loss.
  3. Unit and origin travel with the value. A number is {value, unit, origin}, where origin ∈ INPUT | DERIVED | CATALOG | RULE | UNKNOWN. Adding two values with different units raises rather than silently converting — because a silent foot-to-inch conversion puts a number in a package that nobody entered.
  4. Per-basis units are not convertible. A capacity published "per pair" is lb/pr, and converting it to lb is refused, because in a general-purpose unit library that conversion is a silent no-op that turns a per-pair capacity into a per-beam one. rack-engine/model/quantity.py encodes this as BASIS_BOUND; it is worth carrying over verbatim.

7.4 Canonical serialisation and hashing

The revision's content_hash is SHA-256 over a canonical JSON encoding: sorted keys, fixed number formatting, undefined dropped, explicit units, and a bounded recursion depth that fails loudly rather than truncating — a truncating hash is the one failure mode a hash must not have.

The hash covers content only. Lineage, timestamps, author and note are explicitly excluded, and that exclusion list is held as data so a test can assert it. Two revisions with identical content therefore have identical hashes, which is what makes "did this edit actually change anything?" a one-comparison question and what lets the artifact cache key be sha256(revision_hash ‖ catalog_release ‖ rule_pack ‖ engine_version). Including engine_version in that key is deliberate: a bug fix must invalidate every cached artifact, because that is precisely how you find out which issued documents were affected.

Honest limit

SHA-256 here gives tamper evidence, not tamper proofing. It detects modification to anyone holding a later head hash. It does not stop a database superuser. That is why the head is anchored externally (§13.4) and why customer-facing copy must say "tamper-evident, externally timestamped and independently re-verifiable" and never "tamper-proof".

8.3 Enforcement rules

  1. Every route declares its policy, or the app does not boot. A startup assertion walks the router and fails if any handler lacks an { action, resourceLoader } annotation. This is the one control that survives "someone added an endpoint on a Friday".
  2. Scoped fetch, never fetch-then-check. currentOrg.revisions.find(id), not Revision.find(id) followed by an authorization call. Fetch-then-check fails open when the second line is forgotten; scoped fetch fails closed.
  3. Deny returns 404 for cross-tenant objects. A 403 confirms the object exists.
  4. No mass assignment. Request bodies bind to explicit input DTOs. organization_id, role, audience, lifecycle_state and every price field are structurally unreachable from a request body.
  5. Outbound schema validation. Client-facing response types are declared additionalProperties: false in the OpenAPI schema and validated at runtime — failing the response in non-production, alerting in production.
  6. Idempotency keys on submit, derive, clone and invite. A double-click must not produce two submissions.
  7. Every deny is an audit event, not just a log line.

10Catalog, rule, source and data-provenance strategy

10.1 Two artifacts, two clocks

Part data and standards change on completely different schedules and are approved by different people. They are therefore two independently versioned, independently pinned artifacts:

Catalog release

One manufacturer, one published revision. Parts, geometry, published capacity cases, published footnotes. Sourced from a specific document at a specific page.

Rule pack release

Dimensional conventions, clearance rules, aisle rules, operational rules. Each rule cites a standard, an edition and a section, and carries a verification tier.

A revision pins exactly one of each, and both pins sit inside the hashed content. A catalog change therefore cannot alter a submitted revision — the vocabulary here is Oracle Fusion Configurator's, deliberately: a workspace is drafted, released to produce a numbered version with an effective start date, and the latest version is the one with the latest effective start date, not necessarily the most recently released one.

10.2 Release lifecycle and the two-person rule

DRAFT ──approve──▶ APPROVED ──a newer release──▶ SUPERSEDED ──▶ RETIRED
   ▲                    │
   └── edits allowed    └── immutable; may be pinned by new revisions
  • Only an APPROVED release can be pinned by a new revision. Existing revisions keep their pins forever, including to SUPERSEDED releases, so a two-year-old submission still renders.
  • The approver may not be the digitiser. A machine extraction is evidence, not a signature. A release with a null approved_by fails the build — this gate already exists in rack-app (validate_engineering.py exits non-zero) and it is the reason its beam data can be trusted and its legacy frame data cannot.
  • Superseding a release must list every open request pinned to the old one, before the retire is allowed.
  • Named for MVP-1: one person holds both the catalog-approver and rule-pack-owner roles, with a nominated fallback approver for the case where that person is also the digitiser. Formally accepted for MVP-1 and to be revisited when a second internal engineering resource exists.
  • The approver's procedure is part of the gate, not a convention. A name without a verification act is ceremony — the 72% overstatement was caught by reconciliation, not by a signature. So: the release must carry a recorded verification path as data; the approver independently spot-checks 20 cells or 5% of the table, whichever is greater, with the tool drawing the sample at random (an approver-chosen sample drifts toward the easy cells); any mismatch fails the entire release — there is no partial pass and no "approve with notes"; the approver records sample size, cells, source document and page, and outcome; and the signature attaches to that record. "Approved" with no verification record behind it is not a state the schema can represent.
  • Gate on the verification act, not on the digitiser's identity. A rule of the form "refuse when the approving user equals the digitiser id" is necessary but not sufficient: running an extraction script sets the digitiser to a machine identity, which lets one person approve data they produced themselves and satisfies the check trivially. A machine is a tool, not an independent party. What actually makes the Interlake sets trustworthy is not that a machine typed them — it is that one was cross-checked 357/357 and the other reconciled 435/435 across two independent extraction paths. So the gate should require, for single-human approval, a recorded independent verification path — a full cross-check or a two-path reconciliation, stored as data on the release. Absent that, the release needs a second human. This closes the loophole and it is also what the record should say when someone asks in two years why the table was trusted.
The lesson the reference projects paid for

rack-app/docs/FRAME_VERIFICATION.md records a quarantined frame table indexed on overall frame height under a field labelled height_hbl_in — overstating capacity by up to 72% at HbL 120". It looked completely plausible. It was caught only because two independent extraction paths were reconciled cell by cell (435/435) and because the file's own status field said QUARANTINED until a human signed it. Copy that entire mechanism: status on the data, gate on the status, and a second human in the loop.

10.3 Catalog file format

Declarative JSON or TOML validated against a published schema. Never executable code. rack-engine loads its catalog with runpy.run_path, which executes a Python module as data — defensible for one vendored file in an internal tool, and an arbitrary-code-execution vector the moment a catalog file could be uploaded or edited by anyone outside the core team.

data/catalog/interlake-2026-08/

Fields worth carrying over verbatim from rack-engine/catalog/interlake-2026-08/, because each one was added for a reason:

  • load_basis — the table's own sentence, transcribed. A point load checked against a uniformly-distributed-load chart is not a conservative answer, it is the wrong answer, and only the basis text makes that detectable.
  • deflection_limit and code_basis — so a value can be cited, not just quoted.
  • source_anomalies[] — the manufacturer's own errors, transcribed as published and flagged. The Interlake set has seven: two duplicated part numbers and five 17-character codes where 18 are expected. Silently "fixing" them destroys the ability to reconcile against the source.
  • constraints — published footnotes as data (e.g. bracing required over 126", crossbar required over 90" when decked). Reported, never enforced until an actual check exists, because enforcing a footnote is a check and a check needs a test.

10.4 Lookup semantics

  1. Never interpolate a published capacity table. An off-grid span returns OFF_GRID with both brackets and no value. This is the single most important behaviour in the engine and it is already implemented and tested in both rack-engine and rack-app.
  2. Never nearest-match a part. "Not found" does not mean "probably this one". A part the manufacturer never published cannot be constructed.
  3. Used or generic material gets no capacity, ever. The result is NO_PUBLISHED_CAPACITY naming the part, and the trace shows no table basis at all — quoting one would imply a lookup that never happened.
  4. Basis before number. If the load basis does not match the table's basis, the check returns OUTSIDE_TABLE_BASIS before it looks at any number.
  5. A refusal that leaks a number into the UI is not a refusal. This is worth a test of its own; rack-engine/tests/test_provenance.py has it.

10.5 Rule packs and verification tiers

A rule is not a constant. It is a record with a citation and a confidence, and its confidence caps what it is allowed to conclude. This is the governing decision of the Rack Screening Register Rev B — a separate piece of prior screening-rule research by the product owner, not one of the four reference trees — and it survives into this product unchanged, because it is what keeps the tool honest in front of a client.

Verification tierMeaningMaximum outcome the rule may produce

What this number means for scoping

In that same Rack Screening Register work, 17 of 31 checks reported "Engineering Review Required" purely because the rule was cited from a secondary source rather than from the standard itself. That number is not a defect in the geometry — it is the measure of how much of this product is authority data rather than arithmetic. Buying the standards, or getting a PE to confirm a handful of rules, moves more checks to green than any amount of engineering effort will.

10.6 Provenance model

Adopt the shape of W3C PROV, even though we will never emit RDF. Using the standard's shape forces the field most homegrown designs omit — hadPlan, the slot for which version of the rule was executed.

Three implementation rules make this work rather than becoming a documentation exercise:

  1. The trace is a by-product of evaluation, not a separate logging pass. Each node of the expression tree yields {expr_id, op, operands[], operand_provenance[], value, unit, rounding} as it evaluates. Reconstructing a trace afterwards from logs is how traceability projects fail.
  2. Units are provenance. Dimensional analysis catches a large share of takeoff errors for free, and unit confusion — lineal feet vs square feet vs each — is the classic building-products failure.
  3. Rounding is a versioned rule, not an implementation detail. Record the mode and precision per step. Otherwise "why does the new revision say 118 anchors and the old one said 117" is unanswerable.

10.7 Catalog ingestion lineage

A separate, coarser concern from calculation provenance. Each catalog or price-file load is a run producing a new release; recording it in OpenLineage's Job/Run/Dataset shape gives "where did this value come from" for the ingestion pipeline without polluting the per-value calculation trace. PROV for the calc graph, OpenLineage for the pipeline that feeds it — do not use one for both.

11Validation model

11.1 Severity vocabulary

Seven states. The vocabulary is exact and the words are part of the product — a status that means something slightly different on two screens is worse than no status.

Two failure modes this vocabulary exists to prevent

Silence is not a pass. A check that did not run must say so on the screen, not be absent from it. rack-engine renders STABILITY / SEISMIC / PRELIM — NOT RUN chips unconditionally for exactly this reason.
Missing input is not the same as engineering review. One the client can fix in thirty seconds; the other needs a person with authority. Collapsing them — which the rack-studio prototype does with its five-state vocabulary — makes the client's actionable list unfindable inside a wall of things they cannot act on.

11.2 A finding's outcome is derived from its provenance, never authored

This is the rule that keeps the tool honest, and it was learned the expensive way. An adversarial review of the Rack Screening Register Rev A — prior screening-rule research by the product owner, separate from the four reference trees — found the prose scrupulously honest and the returns dishonest: 14 checks asserted a hard FAIL while the same check's own notes conceded the source had never been read. Five survived as genuinely FAIL-capable.

So the outcome is computed, not typed:

outcome = min( check_result , ceiling_for( rule.verification_tier ) )

# every outcome below is one of the seven states in 11.1 — no check may
# invent an eighth, and the ceiling is applied by the framework, not the check

PRIMARY            -> PASS | BLOCKER
REPRODUCED         -> BLOCKER (carrying a cite-check stamp) | ENGINEERING REVIEW REQUIRED
AHJ_INTERPRETATION -> ENGINEERING REVIEW REQUIRED  (flagged: AHJ confirmation)
SECONDARY          -> ENGINEERING REVIEW REQUIRED
NOT_FOUND          -> NOT EVALUATED

A developer cannot write a check that returns PASS against a secondary-sourced rule, because the ceiling is applied by the framework, not by the check.

11.3 Finding shape

Finding {
  code                  stable enum, e.g. AISLE_CLEAR_SHORTFALL
  severity              from §11.1, derived per §11.2
  subject_object_ids[]  which run / bay / level / aisle it is about
  parameters[]          { name, value, unit, established: bool }
  closed_by             plain-English statement of what would resolve it
  rule_id + rule_pack_release_id      internal only
  citation              standard · edition · section · tier · source note — internal only
  waived_by / waived_at / waiver_reason           internal only, and only for waivable codes
}

Two details matter disproportionately:

  • established on every parameter. An unestablished parameter renders as VERIFY, never as a numeral. A number on a screen is a claim; if the model does not know it, the screen must not print it.
  • closed_by is mandatory. Every finding must be able to say what would close it. A finding with no path to resolution is a dead end, and clients will call about it.

11.5 Overrides and waivers

  • A client can never waive anything. A blocker is a blocker.
  • An internal user can waive only finding codes explicitly marked waivable in the rule pack — never a capacity-table refusal, never an OFF_GRID, never a fire-protection informational.
  • A waiver requires a named acceptor and a reason, becomes an audit event, is stamped on every internal output, and is never applied to a client-visible output.
  • A waiver is scoped to one finding on one revision. It does not travel to a derived revision automatically — the derived revision re-derives and must be waived again if the finding recurs.
  • The authority question — who may waive what — is genuinely undecided (OD-09). Until it is decided, the waiver function should throw an error naming the open decision rather than defaulting to permissive. rack-studio ships exactly this: mayWaive() raises UndecidedAuthorityError naming the ADR item. That is the correct behaviour and it should be copied, not resolved by guessing.

11.6 Assumptions register

An assumption is a stated planning value used in place of a missing input. It is a first-class record, not a footnote:

Assumption {
  key                 e.g. "pallet.overhang.front"
  assumed_value       { value, unit }
  why                 "no pallet overhang supplied; planning default used"
  scope               which objects it affected
  acknowledged_by     the client user who confirmed it at submit
  acknowledged_at
}

Assumptions appear in the pre-submit confirmation, in the client PDF, and at the top of the internal review package. The client's acknowledgement is an audit event and is what makes the internal conversation "you accepted a 4-inch overhang assumption" a fact rather than a recollection.

12BOM and takeoff strategy

12.1 What the BOM is, and is not

In MVP-1 the BOM is an internal takeoff: quantities of frames, beams, decks, spacers, footplates and anchors derived from the submitted configuration, with each quantity traceable to the rule that produced it and the catalog part revision it resolved to. It is not a quote, it is not priced, and it is never shown to a client.

Terminology note: use Sales BOM or Quote BOM, never "SBOM" — as of 2026 that acronym means Software Bill of Materials essentially everywhere and using it here will confuse every technical reader.

The BOM chain, adopting standard PLM view names:

as-configured (client, preliminary, P-revision)
    → as-quoted (internal, released, C-revision, contractual baseline)
        → as-engineered
            → as-installed

MVP-1 builds only the first node — as-configured, plus the internal takeoff derived from it. The as-quoted arrow is phase 3. When it is built, the quote_bom will record derived_from_preliminary_bom_id plus a line-level diff, so an estimator can see exactly what internal review changed from what the client configured — which is both an internal quality signal and the answer to "why is your quote different from what I laid out?"

12.2 Line shape and the one schema decision that cannot be reversed

BomLine {
  bom_run_id
  category            FRAME | BEAM | DECK | SPACER | FOOTPLATE | ANCHOR | ACCESSORY

  -- exactly one of these two is non-null, enforced by a CHECK constraint --
  part_revision_id      ← a REVISION, never a part id.  Non-negotiable.
  uncatalogued_part_id  ← measured geometry, no SKU, no capacity.  half of all jobs.

  qty                 integer  |  UNRESOLVED
  uom                 UN/CEFACT code
  rule_text           the quantity rule in words, e.g.
                      "frames = (bays + 1) x rows; back-to-back rows do not share frames"
  rule_id             + rule_pack_release_id
  confirmed           bool — is this rule established, or a one-job observation?
  item_snapshot       the catalog record, canonicalised and frozen at selection
  source_object_ids[] which runs / bays / levels produced this line
  unresolved_reason   non-null iff qty is UNRESOLVED
}
Two decisions that are cheap now and brutal later

(1) A part reference is a revision, or it is explicitly not a catalog part at all. part_revision_id rather than part_id is how every PLM system versions a line back to a part revision, and it is what makes "we are obsoleting beam SKU 4412 — which open requests break?" a query instead of an archaeology project. The second branch matters just as much here: the job audit found that two of four structural jobs involve material with no published capacity, so a schema where every BOM line must resolve to a catalog part cannot represent half the work. Making that a nullable second reference now is a migration; retrofitting it later is a rewrite of every query that touches a BOM.
(2) Determinism. No now(), no live prices, no unordered iteration, no locale-dependent formatting at calculation time. Every implicit input must become an explicit, recorded one. Both of these are far more expensive to retrofit than to build; everything else in this document can be added later with effort.

12.4 Traceability — from a BOM line back to everything

For any BOM line the system must answer four questions from stored data alone, with no recomputation:

(b) Which catalog part, at which revision?part_revision_id → the frozen item_snapshot and the pinned catalog_release_id, with its source document and page reference.
(c) Which rule version produced the quantity?rule_id + rule_pack_release_id — PROV's hadPlan.

Ship "show your work" as a real internal feature. Clicking any quantity opens the formula in symbols, then the same formula with substituted values and units, the rule that selected the part, and the catalog revision with its effective date. This is the strongest differentiator against spreadsheet-based estimating, and — just as valuably — estimators will find engine bugs with it. The five-section trace panel in rack-engine/rack_app/panels.py is a working reference implementation of exactly this; rebuild it as structured data plus components rather than string-concatenated HTML, and keep every branch, including the branch that shows no table basis at all for used material.

12.5 Regeneration guarantee

The BOM for a submitted revision must be byte-identically regenerable from the revision alone. This is not a nicety; it is the verification technique that makes the record defensible, borrowed directly from reproducible-builds practice:

Reproducible buildsRack Master Studio
Recorded build environmentengine_version, catalog_release_id, rule_pack_release_id, rounding mode, unit system
Deterministic build systemNo now(), no RNG, no map-iteration-order dependence, no floating point in quantities, no network reads at calculation time

A nightly CI job re-renders a sample of historical submissions and asserts byte-identical output. When it fails you have found either an unintended engine change or a leaked implicit input — and that job is itself the documented process evidence that the system "produces an accurate result", which is the standard evidentiary theory for authenticating a computer-generated record.

13Submission, immutability, derived revisions and audit

13.1 What happens on submit

One transaction, in this order. If any step fails, nothing happened.

  1. Re-derive from scratch. The submission is never built from cached derived rows — it re-runs the kernel so that what is frozen is what the model produces, not what a stale cache remembered.
  2. Refuse if any BLOCKER finding is open, listing every reason rather than the first.
  3. Record the client's acknowledgement of the assumption register.
  4. Serialise the submission manifest (below) as canonical JSON and hash it.
  5. Set the revision to frozen; write frozen_at and content_hash.
  6. Persist the derived layout, findings, assumptions and internal BOM keyed to that hash.
  7. Create the submission row with this_hash = SHA-256(prev_hash ‖ manifest_hash ‖ metadata).
  8. Write the audit events.
  9. Enqueue, via the transactional outbox: manifest upload to WORM storage, client PDF generation, notifications.

13.2 The submission manifest

The record of truth is a content-addressed snapshot, not a set of database rows. Rows can be migrated, re-typed and accidentally updated; a canonical document in Object Lock cannot.

submission_manifest {
  schema_version
  project        { id, number, name, organization }
  revision       { id, code, iteration, content_hash, parent, derived_from }
  pins           { catalog_release_id + hash, rule_pack_release_id + hash,
                   engine_version, rounding_mode, unit_system }
  inputs         facility_input, unit_input[], option[], option_parameter[]  (as entered)
  selected       option ids submitted for quote
  derived        layout display list, counts, findings[], assumptions[]
  bom            internal takeoff lines with rule text and part revisions
  acknowledgement{ assumptions_acknowledged_by, at, disclaimer_version_id }
  actor          { user_id, organization_id, at }
}

Serialised with sorted keys, fixed number formatting, explicit units, and a bounded recursion depth that fails loudly. SHA-256 over that text is manifest_hash. The manifest is written to object storage with Object Lock in Compliance mode — where no user, including the account root, can overwrite or delete it before its retention expires.

13.4 Tamper-evident, and saying so accurately

Language discipline — this belongs in every customer-facing claim

Say: "tamper-evident, externally timestamped, and independently re-verifiable."
Never say: "tamper-proof."

A hash chain detects modification to anyone holding a trusted copy of a later head hash. It does not prevent a database superuser from rewriting the chain, and without an external anchor nobody would notice. This is exactly the distinction the Sigstore project states about its own transparency log, and it is the honest framing. Object Lock in Compliance mode is what upgrades one specific artifact — the manifest — from evident to genuinely resistant.

Design the digest exactly the way AWS CloudTrail does rather than inventing a scheme: per-batch SHA-256 hashes, periodic digest files that reference the previous digest, and the head published somewhere the application cannot rewrite. It is a well-tested, auditor-legible design and copying it costs a day.

14Security and tenancy design

14.1 The threat that actually matters

Not an anonymous attacker. The dominant risk in this product is an authenticated, legitimate client user seeing a row or a field they should not — a cost, a margin, a manufacturer part number, a BOM line, another organization's project. Design for "the caller is authenticated and hostile", not "the caller is anonymous". Broken access control remains the top category in the current OWASP Top 10 and was found in every application tested in its dataset.

14.2 Tenant isolation

Shared database, organization_id on every tenant row, Postgres RLS everywhere, and the tenant predicate written explicitly in application queries as well. Database-per-tenant is rejected because internal staff need cross-organization views and that would become an N-database fan-out; schema-per-tenant is rejected as the worst of both — silo's migration cost with pool's shared failure domain. Database-per-tenant remains a config-level escape hatch for a single enterprise client whose procurement demands it, and the tenant-context plumbing is designed now so that would be a configuration change rather than a rewrite.

The two controls are not redundant. They fail differently: RLS fails silently (a SELECT returns empty rather than erroring), application predicates fail loudly in tests. Together you get a quiet net and a loud one.

RLS correctness checklist

#RuleWhy it bites
1Every tenant table has ENABLE and FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY, with a policy covering SELECT, INSERT (WITH CHECK), UPDATE and DELETE.Enabling RLS is default-deny — but a table where you forgot the ALTER TABLE is wide open. 14.3 Invitations

PropertyDecision
Entropy256 bits from a CSPRNG, base64url. Not UUIDv4 — 122 bits, and many libraries use a non-cryptographic RNG.
At restStore sha256(token) only. A database dump then yields no working invitations. A fast hash is correct here precisely because the token is high-entropy; a slow hash is for low-entropy secrets.
Expiry72 hours for invitations, with self-service resend that revokes the prior token. 15 minutes for anything that acts as a login. Note that 72 hours is a deliberate, documented deviation from the 10-minute rule for out-of-band codes, with the 256-bit single-use token as the compensating control.
Single useRedeemed with UPDATE … WHERE accepted_at IS NULL in the same transaction as account creation, checking the affected-row count. Read-then-write lets two concurrent redemptions both succeed.
BindingEmail, organization and role live in the invitation row, never in the token or the URL. The acceptance form must not accept any of them from the client — that is textbook mass assignment.
TransportLand on the acceptance page, immediately POST the token, exchange it for a short-lived server-side session, and 303 to a clean URL. Keeps the token out of browser history, access logs and analytics. Referrer-Policy: no-referrer; no third-party scripts on that page.
No auto-loginRequire an explicit first sign-in with the new credential. It proves the credential works and produces a cleanly audited session-establishment event.
EnumerationExpired, revoked, already-used and nonexistent tokens all render the same page with the same status and comparable timing. Inviting an address that already has an account in another organization must reveal nothing — our customer list is confidential.
NotificationOn acceptance, notify the inviter and the organization owners with IP and time. That is the detection control for a forwarded or stolen invitation.
Rate limitsOn creation (per inviter, per organization — stops invite-spam using our domain reputation) and on redemption (per token, per IP).

14.4 Authentication ladder

WhoMethodRationale
Internal staffMandatory SSO via Microsoft Entra ID (OIDC) with phishing-resistant MFA (passkeys / FIDO2). No local passwords, and no self-service email reset.These accounts see cost, margin and every client's data. Email-based reset on a staff account is a data breach with extra steps; recovery goes through the IdP plus a verified out-of-band human check.
Enterprise clientsSSO via their own IdP, plus SCIM provisioningDeprovisioning is the real prize: when a client fires someone, that access disappears without a support ticket.
Smaller clientsNIST-compliant password plus passkey or TOTP — mandatory, not optionalEmail OTP is a break-glass recovery path only, never the standing second factor.
Magic links

An emailed magic link is not a second factor. NIST's own guidance states that methods which do not prove possession of a specific device — email included — SHALL NOT be used as out-of-band authenticators. For a product holding our BOM and (later) our margin, that means whoever compromises a client's mailbox owns their access. Magic links are acceptable for recovery; they are not acceptable as primary authentication for anyone who can reach internal data, and MVP-1 should not ship them as the default even for clients.

14.5 Authorization

A small hybrid, kept deliberately small:

Relationship layer  (which rows?)   user --member_of{role}--> organization --owns--> project --has--> revision
Role layer          (which verbs?)  5 roles, no more, until a customer demands custom ones
Attribute layer     (which fields?) actor_type ∈ {client, staff, service}
                                     sensitivity ∈ {shared, internal}

Do not adopt an external policy engine yet. With five roles and one relationship chain, OPA / Cedar / OpenFGA add a network hop, a tuple-consistency problem against the database, and an operational surface, for a policy set that fits in one file. Build a single pure authorize(actor, action, resource) function with a table-driven policy, exhaustively unit-tested, enforced by one middleware, structured so the internals could be swapped for Cedar later. Revisit when the policy table exceeds roughly fifty rules or customers demand custom roles.

14.6 Preventing field leakage

Seven layers, ordered by leverage. The goal is to make leakage a test or build failure, never a code-review catch.

  1. Physical separation — internal data in its own tables with an actor_type='staff' policy. Defeats the entire class: accidental SELECT *, a new eager-load, a to_json on a model that grew a column.
  2. Deny-by-default serialization — one DTO per (entity × audience), constructed field by field. Never spread from an entity; never exclude([...]), which is allow-by-default and leaks the next column someone adds.
  3. Outbound schema validation — client response types declared additionalProperties: false; fail in non-production, alert in production.
  4. Input DTOs — the same problem inbound. organization_id, role, audience, lifecycle_state are structurally unreachable from a request body.
  5. Separate bundles — the client application never imports the internal DTO module, so internal field names are not even present in the JavaScript a client downloads.
  6. Contract tests asserting absence — enumerate every client-reachable route automatically, call it as a client principal, and recursively assert no forbidden key appears at any depth. The forbidden list is a single shared constant used by the test, the log redactor and the response validator. Add the positive companion test (staff do see these fields) so an over-eager redaction does not silently break internal tooling.
  7. The impersonation trap — when built, impersonation must replace the principal (actor_type becomes client), with impersonated_by carried for audit only and never influencing an authorization decision. Read-only by default, 30-minute cap, banner-marked, re-auth to start, tenant admin notified.

14.7 SOC 2 posture

Not a v1 requirement, but everything above maps onto the logical-access and monitoring criteria, so building it now makes a later SOC 2 a documentation exercise rather than a re-architecture. Realistic scope when the time comes: Security plus Confidentiality; add Availability only if we sign SLAs; skip Processing Integrity and Privacy for a first report. The items that will not already exist are the written policy set, quarterly access reviews with signed artifacts, a tested restore, an annual penetration test, a vendor/subprocessor list, and one incident-response tabletop with minutes.

17.4 Migration approach

  1. Copy, never move. The four trees stay exactly as they are. Ported code is copied into the new repository and re-tested there under the new boundary rules.
  2. Port the data first. The verified catalog is the most expensive asset and everything else depends on it. It arrives as declarative files with every provenance field intact, and it re-enters DRAFT status until a named second person approves it in the new system — machine reconciliation is evidence, not a signature.
  3. Port assertions before implementations. Write the test first, from the reference project's assertion, then port the code until it passes. An assertion that is not carried over is a claim that quietly stops being checked.
  4. Port the doctrine before either. The twelve rules, the status vocabulary and the source hierarchy are policy documents, and they cost nothing to adopt on day one.
  5. Track every drop. Anything rejected for lack of a source becomes a tracked open item with a named owner, not a silent omission. That register — wire decks, row spacers, footplates, rates — is the roadmap for BOM completeness.
  6. Keep the Carson acceptance case. 916 bays, 6,980 gross, 156 lost, 6,824 net, 324 picking levels, reproduced from a delivered drawing. It is the only end-to-end validation against a real job anywhere in the four projects, and it must go red if the new engine gets counts wrong.

18.3 The two answers that need a second pass

OD-06 — why the storage base changed

The dual display is accepted as answered. The metric storage base cannot be millimetres, and this is arithmetic rather than preference: 48″ is 1219.2 mm, and an integer-mm store rounds it to 1219, which reads back as 47.9921″ and no longer matches the published lookup key. 18 of the 21 published spans miss their own key that way, turning every capacity lookup silently off-grid and destroying the one behaviour the engine is built around.

Integer micrometres solve it: an inch is 25.4 mm exactly, so every published inch value and every whole millimetre lands on a whole µm. That delivers the metric backend the answer asked for with no loss. Mass is asymmetric — the pound converts to metric exactly but not the reverse — so store integer millipounds and render kg as a derived, one-way display value.

OD-17 — answered, and re-cut against the source quotes

A job audit, then a second pass reading the eight source quotations. Two of four structural racking jobs involve material with no published capacity — one entirely used, one mixed. By component count, material value and pallet position the share is about 7%, but a single job is 91% of the volume sample, so those figures are not stable and the job count is the number that should govern: the failure this product can cause is per client, per job, and tonnage is the wrong denominator for a client-experience question.

Used-versus-new is a property of a component, not of a job. The all-used job still purchases new end-of-aisle protectors; the mixed job reuses and buys within the same component type, and its scope block reconciles exactly — reused plus newly purchased equals installed, on every line. There is no job-level flag that would be correct, which is why the reference sits on the BOM line as part_revision_id XOR uncatalogued_part_id and the new uncatalogued_part entity has no capacity column at all. Absent by schema rather than blank by convention, so no future code path can fill it in.

The quotes also produced the clearest evidence yet for the product itself. One job appears across three artifacts — a drawing and two quote revisions — carrying three different pallet-position counts, with a major scope reduction between revisions and nothing linking any of them to a shared model. A second job states two mutually inconsistent position counts on the same line of the same quote. This is the thesis of §1 evidenced from real paperwork rather than argued from first principles.

The matcher is still worth building in Phase 2; the derate still is not. At half of all jobs an estimator aid saying "this is dimensionally consistent with part X — go verify" clearly earns its place. It must not transfer that part's published capacity onto the client's material, with or without a reduction: a used-material derate has no published basis, so it caps at SECONDARY tier, which §11.2's ceiling limits to engineering review required regardless of any click.

The single thing to get right first

Two decisions in this document are structural and get exponentially more expensive to retrofit: BOM lines reference a part revision, never a part, and the derivation is deterministic with every implicit input made explicit. Everything else here — the audit chain, the tier ceilings, the issue register, even the tenant isolation — can be added later with effort. Those two cannot. Build them in week one and the rest of this blueprint stays achievable.
